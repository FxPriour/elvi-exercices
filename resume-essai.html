A. Résumé analytique comparatif – ta copie

Note : 6/20. Remarque de ta professeure : « Les principaux enjeux des deux articles sont cernés, mais vous omettez plus de 10 points et votre plan de RAC est à revoir. Certains passages sont mal compris. Les trop nombreuses fautes entraînent de la confusion et impactent considérablement la note. »

La longueur est bonne : 364 mots pour 350 ± 10 % demandés. Le problème n'est donc pas la quantité, mais ce que tu as choisi de dire.

L'introduction ne fait pas son travail. Elle commence par des généralités qui ne sont pas dans les documents (job displacement, work sectors), puis recopie la question. Il faut au contraire présenter les deux documents (nature, source, date, sujet) et répondre déjà à la question avec une problématique.

Le plan suit les documents plus qu'il ne les compare. Tes connecteurs (First of all, Nevertheless, Moreover, Finally) annoncent des idées, mais chaque paragraphe juxtapose une information du document 1 et une du document 2. Un résumé comparatif organise les idées par thème et dit, pour chacune, ce que les deux documents ont en commun et ce qui les distingue.

Plusieurs passages sont mal compris : « we don't know if AI can be a danger for… higher education » (le document 1 dit clairement le contraire) ; « if teachers learn to use AI more than pupils » (Weissman dit qu'il utilise l'IA « more from the teacher side than from the student side », c'est-à-dire pour préparer ses cours).

Les personnes citées ne sont pas présentées : Alex Karp, Lea Pao et Ian Weissman doivent être présentés en quelques mots (Alex Karp, the CEO of Palantir).

Il n'y a pas de conclusion : la copie s'arrête sur « All in all, ».

B. Essai – ta copie

Sujet : « Do you agree that AI could deepen inequality in education? » (500 mots ± 10 %, avec des exemples du monde anglophone).

Note : 5/20. Remarque de ta professeure : « Les enjeux du sujet sont cernés, mais votre essai est beaucoup trop court ! Attention à la gestion du temps ! Les trop nombreuses fautes et maladresses entraînent de la confusion et impactent très lourdement la note. Copie à retravailler. »

La longueur : le sujet demande 500 mots ± 10 %, soit au moins 450 ; ta copie en compte environ 250, et le nombre de mots n'est pas indiqué. À lui seul, c'est un handicap très lourd : il faut réserver au moins 1 h 30 à l'essai sur les 4 heures.

L'introduction n'a ni accroche ni problématique : « AI has increased in our way to learn » est confus, puis tu donnes ton avis tout de suite (« For me AI could deepen inequality »), sans transition.

Les arguments sont pertinents (fracture numérique, inégalité dans l'accompagnement à l'école, dépendance), mais ils reposent uniquement sur les documents 3 et 4, qu'ils paraphrasent. La consigne dit au contraire de réagir au dossier « sans paraphraser celui-ci ».

Il manque des exemples du monde anglophone : la consigne exige des exemples « culturels, civilisationnels et/ou historiques du monde anglophone ». Ta copie n'en contient aucun.

Il manque une nuance : on attend aussi l'argument inverse (l'IA peut réduire les inégalités, avec un tuteur personnel pour tous), puis une prise de position argumentée.

La conclusion se réduit à une phrase inachevée et confuse.

La langue

Les erreurs de langue de ces deux copies sont pour la plupart déjà travaillées dans la fiche de grammaire : modal + base verbale (can creates, can cures, could better understood, how to used), propositions en wh- (where are you living, how can we study, how can they use), accords (others teachers, less abilities, homeworks, he teachs), prépositions (an impact the, in a smarter way), gallicismes (connexion, explication, IA, a price).

Nouveaux points : however (= cependant) et non although (= bien que) en début de phrase ; the internet avec l'article ; the importance of (nom) et non the important of ; claim that pour rapporter une opinion ; attention aux temps du discours rapporté (Other teachers think there could be…, et non said there would have been).

Résumé : un plan comparatif possible

Le plan est organisé par thème, et chaque partie compare les deux documents.

Introduction

  • Present both documents: document 1, an article from The Guardian (March 2026) quoting American humanities professors; document 2, from The New York Times (June 2026), about a Manhattan high school teacher who won a prize for teaching with AI.
  • Answer the question at once: both see AI as a real threat, but the danger depends on who controls the tool.

I. A threat to learning and thinking

  • Doc 1: reliance on AI is incompatible with developing human intelligence; students use it instead of thinking; studies point to a decline in reading, analysis and synthesis (Michael Clune).
  • Doc 2: even Ian Weissman, who uses AI, does not want it to replace students' own work.
  • Comparison: shared concern, but far more alarmist in doc 1.

II. A threat to institutions

  • Doc 1 goes further: the purpose of costly university degrees is questioned; the humanities could disappear (Alex Karp, CEO of Palantir) or become the privilege of an elite.
  • Doc 2: the threat is felt by families: an AI-focused high school scrapped, a petition for a two-year moratorium.
  • Comparison: universities and humanities in doc 1, secondary school and parents in doc 2.

III. Yet a useful tool if teachers stay in control

  • Doc 1: productivity in the sciences; Daniela Amodei (Anthropic) thinks the humanities will matter more than ever.
  • Doc 2: AI used “from the teacher side” enriches lessons (Berlin Conference, World War I trenches).
  • Common point: teachers lack guidance (“left to their own devices” / “the Wild West”), despite New York's first AI playbook.

Conclusion

  • One sentence: AI threatens education less as a technology than as a substitute for thinking, so teachers must stay in charge.
Résumé : a model summary

Two articles published in 2026 examine how artificial intelligence is affecting education in the United States: document 1, from the British daily The Guardian, reports the views of American university professors in the humanities, while document 2, from The New York Times, portrays a Manhattan high school teacher who won a prize for teaching with AI. Both see AI as a real threat, but suggest that the danger depends on who controls the tool.

Both documents first present AI as a threat to learning itself. According to the professors interviewed in document 1, relying on AI is incompatible with developing human intelligence: students use it instead of thinking, and early studies point to a decline in their ability to read, analyse and synthesise. Dora Zhang, a literature professor at Berkeley, even wonders what AI is doing “to us as a species”. Even Ian Weissman, the teacher in document 2, is uncomfortable with the idea of AI replacing students' own work.

The threat also concerns institutions. Document 1 goes further, suggesting that the very purpose of expensive American degrees is in question and that the humanities could disappear, as Alex Karp, the CEO of Palantir, predicts, or survive only for an elite while others receive “soulless” training from AI instructors. In document 2, the threat is felt by families: in New York, plans for an AI-focused high school were scrapped after parents called for a two-year moratorium.

However, neither document sees AI as an inevitable danger. Scientists praise its “productivity boost”, and Daniela Amodei, the president of Anthropic, believes the humanities will matter more than ever. Document 2 shows that AI used “from the teacher side” can enrich lessons, letting students question figures from the Berlin Conference or experience the trenches of World War I, even if such projects take time and must respect historical accuracy. Yet both stress that teachers lack guidance: professors are left “to their own devices”, and Weissman calls regulation “the Wild West”.

AI therefore threatens education less as a technology than as a substitute for thinking, which is why both documents want teachers to stay in charge.

Essai : un plan détaillé possible

Les exemples viennent de l'actualité et de la civilisation du monde anglophone : c'est ce type de références que le jury attend, plus qu'une culture spécialisée.

Introduction (≈ 80 words)

  • Hook: in 2023, Khan Academy launched Khanmigo, an AI tutor meant to give every pupil the kind of personal help once reserved for the wealthy.
  • Problématique: will AI mainly benefit those who are already advantaged, or can it become a tool for equal opportunity?
  • Announce the plan.

I. Yes: AI risks deepening existing inequalities (≈ 170 words)

  • The digital divide: devices and connection. Example: the “homework gap” revealed when American schools closed during the Covid-19 pandemic (pupils doing homework in fast-food car parks to use the wifi).
  • Unequal guidance: some pupils are taught to use AI critically, others are left alone with it. Example: New York City's public schools banned ChatGPT in early 2023, then lifted the ban a few months later; schools still have very different policies.
  • A two-tier education: human teachers for an elite, screens for the rest. Examples: Silicon Valley executives who send their own children to screen-free schools; the gap between Britain's private schools, such as Eton, and state schools.

II. But AI could also reduce inequality (≈ 170 words)

  • A personal tutor for everyone: private tutoring is expensive, but AI tutors such as Khanmigo could make individual help available to all.
  • Help for pupils who struggle: explanations at their own pace, translation, accessibility for pupils with disabilities.
  • Technology plus public policy can widen access to knowledge. Example: the public libraries funded by Andrew Carnegie in the United States and Britain.

Conclusion (≈ 80 words)

  • Answer the question: AI could deepen inequality, but it does not have to; it is an amplifier.
  • What makes the difference: equipment for all, AI literacy taught at school, teachers kept at the centre.
  • Opening: the real question is political rather than technological.
Essai : a model introduction

In 2023, Khan Academy launched Khanmigo, an AI tutor meant to give every pupil the kind of personal help once reserved for the wealthy. Yet access to computers, connections and good teaching is far from equal. Will AI, like many technologies before it, mainly benefit those who are already advantaged, or can it become a tool for equal opportunity? While AI may well deepen existing inequalities, it could also reduce them, provided that schools and governments make it accessible to all.
